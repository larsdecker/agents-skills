# Content Intent and Citeability

Assess whether a page answers a real information need and whether its key passages can be understood and evaluated in context.

## Page intent record

For each representative page, record:

| Field | Question |
|---|---|
| Primary topic | What is the page substantially about? |
| Primary intent | What is the visitor trying to learn or do? |
| Primary question | What direct question should this page answer? |
| Secondary questions | What related decisions or constraints matter? |
| Answer type | Explanation, procedure, comparison, recommendation, reference, or other? |
| Coverage | Does the page answer the question, and where? |

Do not substitute keyword frequency for intent coverage.

## Passage review

Review three to five important passages for:

- **Answer completeness**: can the passage answer its question?
- **Semantic independence**: are the subject and context clear when quoted alone?
- **Specificity**: does it say something concrete and bounded?
- **Evidence**: can the reader inspect the basis for the claim?
- **Scope**: are conditions, limitations, and uncertainty clear?
- **Freshness**: are dates, versions, or time-sensitive details current and visible?
- **Attribution**: is it clear who is making the claim?

Treat sentence length, context-dependent openers, counts of numbers, question headings, and list/table presence as review prompts only. They are weak signals and often meaningless on navigation, overview, narrative, or short pages. Never report thresholds as ranking factors.

## Original information gain

Look for first-party data, actual tests, reproducible measurements, original examples, case studies, methodology, limitations, version details, and named expert experience. Distinguish supported results from vague claims such as “our tests showed great results.” Content that restates common knowledge without evidence or useful perspective may have little information gain; this is a qualitative comparison, not an automatic penalty.
